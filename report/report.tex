\section*{Summary}
Arcuschin et al.\ (ICML 2026) ask models pairs of yes/no questions that differ only in the order of two entities, such as ``Is X south of Y?'' and ``Is Y south of X?''. Exactly one is true. Some models answer both the same way, in the direction they lean for that kind of question, while each chain of thought looks sound on its own. The authors call this \emph{implicit post-hoc rationalization} (IPHR). Their Figure 2 reports the share of pairs flagged this way for 16 models, from 13.5\% (GPT-4o-mini) to 0.04\% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet with thinking).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Released data.} Our code gives the plotted counts exactly for the 8 models whose 10-answer counts were released, as well as their 64 values in the paper's Table 10 (rates over other cutoffs). We found three discrepancies in the paper. Figure 2 divides by 4,892 pairs where the data hold 4,834. The 100-answer retest uses a 0.4 cutoff where the text states 0.5; at 0.5, 61\% of flags survive on average (74\% at the code's 0.4) against the reported 76.5\%. The text says the model ranking holds at every cutoff, while its own Table 10 shows swaps.
\item \textbf{Fresh runs.} On 1,014 pairs with the paper's prompt and settings, each of the four models (Gemini 2.5 Flash in its general release) has a lean gap within 0.012 of the paper's. Flag counts agree for GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.3 70B. DeepSeek V3 gets 6 against the paper's 12 under the same judge; its leans sit near the cutoff. Only 1 of its 12 flags recurs, below the 14--80\% expected for an unchanged model, though the evidence is weak. Pair-level asymmetry beyond the template lean repeats across runs in both directions, so it is a stable property of the pairs; our test does not tie it to the lean direction.
\item \textbf{By kind of question.} Geography leans NO and other questions lean YES. The top and bottom of the model ranking swap between the two: GPT-4o-mini is flagged on 19.6\% of geography pairs and 1.4\% of the rest, QwQ on 0.2\% and 13.6\%.
\item \textbf{Extensions.} Printing both true values removes the flags, as expected. Asked for each value alone, Llama 3.3 orders 72\% of its flagged non-geography pairs correctly (chance is 50\%, unflagged pairs 85\%). On geography, GPT-4o-mini's flags are most common for places far apart. In our run one sentence on how to read the direction word moves its geography flags from 131 to 31 of 684. The opposite sentence moves them to 667. Much of GPT-4o-mini's Figure 2 rate depends on how it reads the direction word, though the paper's same-longitude ablation still flags it on 9.1\% of pairs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The metric}
Each of 58 templates (29 properties $\times$ ``greater'' or ``less'') holds pairs of a YES-question and its reversed NO-question. The authors ask each question 10 times at temperature 0.7 with the prompt ``Here is a question with a clear YES or NO answer \ldots{} think step by step, and only then give a YES / NO answer.'' A judge labels each answer YES, NO or UNKNOWN. An UNKNOWN counts as half a YES. A pair is flagged when (i) the two accuracies differ by at least 0.5, (ii) the template's mean YES rate is at least 0.05 from 0.5 and (iii) the less accurate question's correct answer is opposite to that lean. We implement the criteria from the authors' code (\texttt{make\_faithfulness\_ds.py}). It differs from the paper's text in two places. It lowers the accuracy cutoff to 0.4 for questions with more than 10 answers. It also counts REFUSED and ``NO because the values are equal'' as UNKNOWN.

\section{Reproduction from the released data}
The authors' repository holds per-question YES/NO/UNKNOWN counts for all 16 models. For the 8 models sampled 10 times per question, our implementation gives exactly the plotted counts (e.g.\ 660 for GPT-4o-mini) and their 64 values in the paper's Table 10. For the other 8, the authors later drew 100 answers for each flagged pair and released only those. For these we can check only that the number of pairs with 100 answers equals the plotted count, which holds for all 8. Figure~\ref{fig:repro}a shows the bars over 4,834 pairs, the denominator Table 10 uses, which raises each rate by 1.2\% of its value. The paper reports that 76.5\% of flags survive 100 answers per question, averaged over models. Averaged the same way, we get 74\% at the code's 0.4 cutoff and 61\% at the stated 0.5 (our retest counts for ChatGPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro differ from the paper's Table 4). The paper's text says the ranking of models holds at all 20 cutoff settings. Over the same 20 settings for the 8 checkable models, neighbours swap places in 16 of the 19 non-default settings (Kendall $\tau \geq 0.857$). The paper's Table 10 shows such swaps too. The template-cluster intervals in Figure~\ref{fig:repro}a overlap for most mid-ranked models, so the data do not fix their order.

\section{Fresh runs}
\textbf{Setup.} We drew 21\% of each template's pairs with a fixed seed: 1,014 pairs from 56 templates (two templates on the area of populated places have 2 pairs each and contribute none), or 20,280 answers per model. On the authors' data this sample moves rates by $-0.4$ to $+1.0$ points, because leans estimated from fewer questions cross the 0.05 cutoff more often. So we always compare with the paper's run on the same pairs. We used the authors' prompt word for word. Like their code, we sent top-p 0.9 to GPT-4o-mini and none to Llama 3.3, DeepSeek V3 and Gemini 2.5 Flash; four newer models got the stated top-p 0.9. Thinking models got 8,000 tokens; 3.2\% of Qwen 3.8 Flash's answers still ran out and count as UNKNOWN. The paper used a preview of Gemini 2.5 Flash; we used the general release, which returned no thinking tokens.

\textbf{Labels.} The paper's judge, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, is retired. A strict text rule labels an answer whose last three lines hold one bare YES or NO and which contains no hedging words. Claude Haiku 5.5 labels the rest with the authors' judge prompt. To separate the judge from the model, we relabelled the paper's own answers on our pairs. Our labels agree with theirs on 98.2--99.9\% of answers. The differences mostly run one way: answers such as ``I cannot verify the dates, so NO'' are UNKNOWN under the judge prompt. The paper's judge often called them NO. This gives our labeller 12 fewer flags for GPT-4o-mini (134 vs 146) and 3 more for Llama 3.3 (31 vs 28). Claude Haiku 5.5 also labels its own answers, which may lower its measured rate.

\textbf{Results.} Each model's lean gap differs from the paper's by at most 0.012. The ratio of our flag count to the paper's (same judge) has an interval containing 1 for all four models; it is narrow for GPT-4o-mini (139 vs 134) and wide for the rest. Our judge accounts for 5 of DeepSeek V3's drop from 17 to 6 flags. At a 0.10 cutoff the runs give 2 and 3 flags, so its leans sit near the 0.05 cutoff. Four newer models give 1--13 flags (0.1--1.3\%). The share of the paper's flags that recur in our run is close to what an unchanged model would give for GPT-4o-mini and Gemini 2.5 Flash, above it for Llama 3.3 and below it for DeepSeek V3 (1 of 12, against an expected 45\%, interval 14--80\%). With four models compared, a McNemar $p$ of 0.21 and a ratio interval that contains 1, this is weak evidence of a change. Its residual correlation (0.41) points to the same pair-level behaviour. OpenRouter routed its calls to two providers.

\section{What produces the flags}
\textbf{Pair-level asymmetry repeats.} Suppose a model knows a pair with probability $k$ and answers both orders correctly; otherwise it answers YES with probability $b$ shared by the whole template. Its accuracies are then $k + (1-k)b$ and $k + (1-k)(1-b)$, with no order effect beyond the template lean. We fit $k$ per pair and $b$ per template. Each pair's leftover asymmetry (its accuracy difference minus $(1-k)(2b-1)$) should then be noise and should not correlate between two independent runs. It correlates at 0.33--0.69 against null 97.5th percentiles of 0.17--0.22 (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}b). This can come from an order effect specific to the pair or from stable pair-level knowledge, such as a consistent wrong belief. To separate them we selected the pairs whose leftover asymmetry in the paper's run lies above the null's 97.5th percentile in the lean direction. As a control we selected the pairs below its 2.5th percentile. Both groups return in our run by similar amounts (GPT-4o-mini: 0.19 on the lean side, 0.24 on the other side, nulls 0.04 and 0.05; Table~\ref{tab:agree}). So the pairs differ in a stable way, but the test does not single out the lean direction. The template-only model also falls short of the observed flag count for all 8 checkable models in the paper (sign test $p = 0.008$); on our smaller runs it falls short only for GPT-4o-mini (139 vs 106--129). Neither result shows that the chains of thought are post-hoc.

\textbf{By kind of question.} Of the 1,989 pairs flagged with 10 answers per question for the 8 checkable models (one topped-up Gemini 2.5 Flash pair left out here, though the figure includes it), 1,269 are geography questions in templates leaning NO and 590 are other questions in templates leaning YES; Gemini 1.5 Pro has the most exceptions (94 geography flags leaning YES). Models differ mostly in which kind they fail on (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}a). Geography is two thirds of the dataset, so Figure 2's ranking largely reflects geography.

\section{Extensions}
\textbf{1. Values in the prompt.} With both entities' true values printed above the unchanged question (e.g.\ ``Lord Sunday: 2010-01-30; The Scarlet Gospels: 2015-05-17''), accuracy is 99.5--100\% and flags fall to 0--2 for three original models and GPT-5.6 Luna (Figure~\ref{fig:ext}a; non-geography 57 to 0). This was expected, since the prompt holds the answer. We also printed one rounded value for both entities on the 330 non-geography pairs (e.g.\ ``both released around 2006''), which leaves the order open. Most answers then say the two values are the same and answer NO in both orders, which the judge prompt counts as UNKNOWN: 98\% for GPT-4o-mini and 89\% for Llama 3.3. The rates stay the same when we add ``You must answer YES or NO.'' The few remaining YES and NO answers are too few to measure a lean. Asked for each value alone, with no comparison (3 samples), Llama 3.3 put the two entities in the right order for 72\% of its 32 flagged non-geography pairs, against 85\% for unflagged pairs and 50\% for guessing (GPT-4o-mini: 5 of 8 and 83\%). The 72\% has a 95\% interval of 55--84\%; it is above guessing ($p = 0.02$) and the gap to unflagged pairs is not significant ($p = 0.08$). In the comparison, its answers state the wrong order in 55\% of answers to the question it gets wrong and in 5\% to the reversal. The values it writes match its answer, as in the paper's ``biased fact inconsistency''.

\textbf{2. Geography wording.} 131 of GPT-4o-mini's 139 flags in our run are geography. Its geography flags rise with the distance between the places on the asked axis, from 10\% of pairs in the nearest quartile to 42\% in the farthest, where accuracy on YES-questions falls to 55\% (the paper's data: 9\% to 44\%). Far-apart places should be the easiest to order, so missing knowledge is unlikely to be the main cause, although 30\% of the wrong answers across its 131 flagged geography pairs state the wrong order. We asked GPT-4o-mini's 684 geography pairs six more ways (Figure~\ref{fig:ext}b). A paraphrase that keeps the vague direction gives 140 flags. Adding one sentence to the paper's question, ``Count X as west of Y if it is anywhere further west, even if it is also far to the north or south'', gives 31. Adding the opposite sentence, ``only if it is directly west of it, at about the same latitude'', gives 667 (with the direction word changed for north, south and east templates): under that reading NO is right for both orders. The metric still counts this consistent behaviour as unfaithful. Accuracy on YES-questions is 77\% with the paper's wording, 94\% and 12\% with the two sentences; accuracy on NO-questions is 97\%, 91\% and 99\%. So the first sentence also pushes answers towards YES in general. It also raises the share of answers citing coordinates from 46\% to 87\%. The ``more western longitude'' wording (24 flags, NO accuracy 94\%) is the cleaner disambiguation. The asked coordinate of both places (Extension 1's card) gives 2 flags. Both coordinates of both places give 0. Strict-reading phrases (``directly west'', ``different continents'') appear in 23\% of answers to the YES-question of flagged pairs, 15\% to its reversal and 4--5\% in unflagged pairs. A typical answer: ``Since Sahuarita Lake is southwest of Big Stone Lake, it is not directly west of it. Based on this reasoning, the answer is NO.'' No answer we read says the question can be read two ways.

\section{What this establishes and what remains open}
\textbf{Established.} Figure 2's counts follow from the released data for the 8 models we can check. On the same pairs more than a year later, each model's lean gap differs from the paper's by at most 0.012. Pair-level asymmetry beyond the template lean repeats across runs, well above sampling noise, though our tests cannot tie it to the lean direction. Flags split by kind of question, geography leaning NO and other questions leaning YES, so Figure 2's ranking depends on the dataset's mix. GPT-4o-mini's geography flags depend strongly on how the direction word is read, a reading the paper's ambiguity filter did not remove.

\textbf{Open.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Faithfulness or ambiguity on geography.} The paper counts its Figure 1 example, a strict reading of ``south of'', as switching arguments. A model that read ``west of'' strictly in both orders would be consistent. GPT-4o-mini's behaviour is partly a consistent strict reading and partly a switch: strict-reading phrases appear in both orders, in 15\% of answers to the NO-question and 23\% to the YES-question, where that reading changes the answer. In the paper's same-longitude ablation, where the strict reading cannot apply, GPT-4o-mini is still flagged on 9.1\% of pairs.
\item \emph{Same weights.} OpenRouter routed our DeepSeek V3 calls to two providers and our Llama calls to nine. We cannot check that the weights match the paper's.
\item \emph{Judges and small numbers.} We checked our labels against the authors' on 20,280 answers per original model. We checked the fact reader only by reading samples. The recall results rest on 32 Llama pairs and 8 GPT-4o-mini pairs outside geography; the fact-reading results add GPT-4o-mini's 131 flagged geography pairs. Whether the values written in a comparison drive its answer or follow it remains open.
\item \emph{Scope.} One prompt, one dataset of obscure entities, 1,014 pairs, one model for the wording study. We did not test the paper's math-shortcut result.
\end{itemize}
For monitoring, a single GPT-4o-mini answer in a flagged pair rarely states uncertainty (3\% of wrong geography answers, none of 64 elsewhere), so its inconsistency shows only when the two orders are compared. Llama states uncertainty in 89\% of its wrong non-geography answers and answers anyway.

The runs cost \$103 on OpenRouter. \textbf{Use of AI tools.} Claude (Anthropic) wrote most of the code and drafts of this report under our direction. AI reviewers critiqued the drafts. Table 1 is generated from the result files.
