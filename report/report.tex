\section*{Summary}
Arcuschin et al.\ (ICML 2026) ask models pairs of yes/no questions that differ only in the order of two entities, such as ``Is X south of Y?'' and ``Is Y south of X?''. Exactly one is true. Some models answer both the same way, in the direction they lean for that kind of question, while each chain of thought looks sound on its own. The authors call this \emph{implicit post-hoc rationalization} (IPHR). Their Figure 2 reports the share of 4,834 pairs flagged this way for 16 models, from 13.5\% (GPT-4o-mini) to 0.04\% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet with thinking).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Released data.} Our code reproduces the flagged counts exactly for the 8 models whose 10-answer counts were released. We found two discrepancies. The plot divides by 4,892 pairs where the data hold 4,834. The 100-answer retest uses a 0.4 cutoff where the paper states 0.5; at 0.5, 61\% of flags survive where the paper reports 76\%.
\item \textbf{Fresh runs.} On 1,014 of the pairs with the paper's prompt and settings, the size of each model's lean reproduces for all four original models still served. The flag rate reproduces for GPT-4o-mini (13.7\% vs 14.4\%) and Llama 3.3 70B (3.5\% vs 2.8\%). DeepSeek V3's rate falls (0.6\% vs 1.7\%) because its leans sit at the cutoff. For GPT-4o-mini and Llama, single flags recur as often as 10 answers per question allow.
\item \textbf{What produces the flags.} A model that either knows a pair or guesses with one fixed lean per template predicts the flag count for half of the paper's checkable models and for all eight of our runs. The leans split by kind of question. Geography leans NO; dates, sizes and ages lean YES.
\item \textbf{Extensions.} With both true values printed above the question, flags fall to 0--2 for four models. Asking GPT-4o-mini's geography questions as ``a more western longitude'', with no values given, cuts its flags from 131 to 24. Most of the tallest bar in Figure 2 comes from a strict reading of ``west of'' that the model applies without saying so.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The metric}
Each of 58 templates (29 properties $\times$ ``greater'' or ``less'') holds pairs of a YES-question and its reversed NO-question. The authors ask each question 10 times at temperature 0.7 with the prompt ``Here is a question with a clear YES or NO answer \ldots{} think step by step, and only then give a YES / NO answer.'' A judge labels each answer YES, NO or UNKNOWN. An UNKNOWN counts as half a YES. A pair is flagged when (i) the two accuracies differ by at least 0.5, (ii) the template's mean YES rate is at least 0.05 from 0.5 and (iii) the less accurate question's correct answer is opposite to that lean. We implement the criteria from the authors' code (\texttt{make\_faithfulness\_ds.py}). It differs from the paper's text in two places. It lowers the accuracy cutoff to 0.4 for questions with more than 10 answers. It also counts REFUSED and ``NO because the values are equal'' as UNKNOWN.

\section{Reproduction from the released data}
The authors' repository holds per-question YES/NO/UNKNOWN counts for all 16 models. For the 8 models sampled 10 times per question, our implementation gives exactly the plotted counts (e.g.\ 660 for GPT-4o-mini, 363 for Claude 3.5 Haiku). For the other 8 models the authors later drew 100 answers for each flagged pair and released only those. Here we can only check bookkeeping: the number of topped-up pairs equals the plotted count for all 8. Figure~\ref{fig:repro}a shows the bars over 4,834 pairs, which raises each rate by 1.2\% of its value. Changing the two cutoffs over $\{0.03, 0.05, 0.07, 0.10\}\times\{0.4, 0.5, 0.6\}$ leaves the ranking of the 8 checkable models almost unchanged (Kendall $\tau \geq 0.86$), as the paper reports. The retest is less clean. The paper reports that 76.5\% of flags survive 100 answers per question, averaged over models. Averaged the same way, we get 74\% at the code's 0.4 cutoff (two models' denominators differ from the paper's Table 4) and 61\% at the stated 0.5 cutoff.

\section{Fresh runs}
\textbf{Setup.} We drew 21\% of each template's pairs with a fixed seed: 1,014 pairs from 56 templates (the two templates on the area of populated places have 2 pairs each and contribute none), or 20,280 answers per model. On the authors' data this sample moves rates by $-0.4$ to $+1.0$ points, because leans estimated from fewer questions cross the 0.05 cutoff more often. We therefore always compare with the paper's run on the same pairs. We used the authors' prompt word for word and copied what their code sent: top-p 0.9 for OpenAI models, none for models served through OpenRouter. Thinking models got 8,000 tokens; 3.2\% of Qwen 3.8 Flash's answers still ran out and count as UNKNOWN. Gemini 2.5 Flash is now the general release (the paper used a preview) and reported no thinking tokens.

\textbf{Labels.} The paper's judge, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, is retired. We accept an answer by a strict text rule when its last three lines hold one bare YES or NO and it contains no hedging words. Everything else goes to Claude Haiku 5.5 with the authors' judge prompt. On 1,200 random answers from the authors' release our labels match theirs in 98.3\% (GPT-4o-mini) and 99.8\% (Claude 3.5 Haiku) of cases. To measure the effect on flags, we relabelled the paper's own answers on our 1,014 pairs for the four original models (Table~\ref{tab:agree}). Our labeller agrees on 98.2--99.9\% of answers and finds fewer flags. For GPT-4o-mini it finds 134 where the paper's labels give 146, all of them among the paper's. Most differences are answers such as ``I cannot verify the dates, so NO'' or ``south of does not apply across continents, NO''. The judge prompt classes these as UNKNOWN; the paper's judge often called them NO. Claude Haiku 5.5 also labels its own answers, which may flatter its rate.

\textbf{Results.} The lean gap, which needs no cutoff, matches within 0.012 for all four models (Table~\ref{tab:agree}). Flag counts match for GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.3 (McNemar $p = 0.49$ and $0.23$). DeepSeek V3's lean is unchanged but its templates sit near the 0.05 cutoff. At 0.10 the two runs give 2 and 3 flags; at 0.05 they give 17 and 6. The judge accounts for 5 of those 11 pairs (17 to 12 on the paper's own answers). Four newer models give 0.1--1.3\% (Figure~\ref{fig:repro}b).

\textbf{Do single flags recur?} Each flag rests on 20 answers, so even an unchanged model would not flag the same pairs twice. We simulated two independent 10-answer runs from the paper's per-question rates to get a ceiling. GPT-4o-mini's flags recur at the ceiling (72\% vs 69\%) and Llama's above it (68\% vs 54\%). DeepSeek V3 (6\% vs 45\%) and Gemini 2.5 Flash (48\% vs 63\%) fall below, which fits a changed checkpoint or provider. A single flag is a noisy reading of a stable property.

\section{What produces the flags}
\textbf{A model with no order effect.} Suppose a model knows a pair with probability $k$ and then answers both orders correctly. Otherwise it guesses YES with probability $b$, the same for every pair in a template. The two accuracies are then $k + (1-k)b$ and $k + (1-k)(1-b)$. This model has no effect of question order beyond the template lean. We fit $k$ per pair and $b$ per template, simulated 200 runs and counted flags (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}a). It predicts the observed count for four of the paper's models (GPT-4o-mini: 660 vs 653, interval 625--679; Llama 3.1; Llama 3.3; Gemini 2.5 Flash) and for all eight of our runs. Gemini 1.5 Pro is just above its interval. Claude 3.5 Haiku (363 vs 310), QwQ (220 vs 176) and DeepSeek V3 (60 vs 37) have 15--40\% more flags than a template lean explains, so pair-specific effects may exist there.

\textbf{Two kinds of lean.} Of the 1,989 pairs flagged for the 8 models with 10-answer data, 1,269 are geography questions in templates that lean NO and 590 are other questions in templates that lean YES. Gemini 1.5 Pro is the main exception, with 94 geography flags leaning YES. GPT-4o-mini's 660 flags are 638 geography and 22 other; Llama 3.3's are 7 and 95.

\section{Extensions}
\textbf{1. Fact cards.} We printed both entities' true values from the dataset above the unchanged question (e.g.\ ``Lord Sunday: 2010-01-30; The Scarlet Gospels: 2015-05-17'') and ran all 1,014 pairs again on three original models and one new one. Flags fall from 139 to 2 for GPT-4o-mini and to 0 for Llama 3.3, DeepSeek V3 and GPT-5.6 Luna, with accuracy of 99.5--100\% (Figure~\ref{fig:ext}a). This does not separate the paper's two explanations, order-dependent recall and a lean applied under uncertainty, since both predict it. It shows that the lean does not act on values the model can read, as the know-or-guess model predicts for $k \to 1$.

\textbf{2. Coordinate wording.} Geography makes up 94\% of GPT-4o-mini's flags in our run. We asked its 684 geography pairs again with no values, replacing ``located west of'' by ``at a more western longitude than'' (and likewise for the other directions). Flags fall from 131 to 24 (Figure~\ref{fig:ext}b). Accuracy on questions whose answer is YES rises from 77\% to 96\%. The mean template lean moves from $-0.095$ to $+0.007$. The model has the same knowledge under both wordings, so most of its geography flags come from how it reads the question.

\textbf{What the wrong answers state.} Claude Haiku 5.5 read every answer in the flagged pairs of GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.3. For each, it reported which entity the answer's own statements place higher on the asked property, whether the conclusion rests on a different property and whether the answer says it is unsure (Table~\ref{tab:facts}). We checked it by reading samples and narrowed its questions after a first version counted north/south statements on east/west questions. In GPT-4o-mini's geography answers, 44\% of wrong answers state the right order and conclude the opposite. A typical one reads: ``Since Sahuarita Lake is southwest of Big Stone Lake, it is not directly west of it. Based on this reasoning, the answer is NO.'' Under that reading NO is right for both orders, yet the model never says it chose the reading. On other questions the wrong answers state a wrong order: GPT-4o-mini in all 64 of its wrong answers (8 pairs), Llama 3.3 in 78\% of 230. The stated order depends on the question order. Llama states the wrong order in 55\% of answers to the question it gets wrong and in 5\% to its reversal. Llama says it is unsure in 89\% of these wrong answers and then commits; GPT-4o-mini almost never does. Most stated orders are positions or rough dates, not exact figures.

\section{What this establishes and what remains open}
\textbf{Established.} Figure 2's counts follow from the released data. Its ranking of models survives the threshold choices. The size of each model's lean repeats more than a year later. The flags are well described as a template-level guess applied when the model does not know. They vanish when the values are in view. For GPT-4o-mini, the tallest bar, most flags come from a strict reading of ``west of'' and ``south of'' that the paper's ambiguity filter let through. In both cases the chain of thought presents reasons and leaves out the lean or the reading. A single trace cannot show either; asking the reversed question can.

\textbf{Uncertain.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Faithfulness or ambiguity.} A model that reads ``west of'' as ``directly west'' in both orders is consistent, which the paper's definition would not call unfaithful. In GPT-4o-mini's flagged geography pairs, phrases such as ``directly west'' or ``different continents'' appear in 23\% of answers to the question whose answer is YES and in 15\% of answers to its reversal (4--5\% in unflagged pairs). So the model uses the reading in both orders, more often where it changes the answer. The paper's same-longitude ablation (9.1\% for GPT-4o-mini) suggests the reading is not the whole story.
\item \emph{Same model, same weights.} OpenRouter routed our DeepSeek V3 calls to two providers and our Llama calls to nine. We cannot verify that the weights match the paper's.
\item \emph{Judges.} Labels and fact readings come from Claude Haiku 5.5. Labels were checked against the authors' on 20,280 of their answers for each original model. The fact reader was checked only by reading samples, not by blind human labels.
\item \emph{Scope.} One prompt, one dataset of obscure entities, 1,014 pairs. Low rates rest on a few flags. The paper's other result, on math shortcuts, is untouched.
\end{itemize}
The runs cost \$76 on OpenRouter: \$61 for answers, \$14 for labels and \$2 for the fact reader. The repository holds the code, all labels, per-pair results, the decision log and the raw answers as a release.
